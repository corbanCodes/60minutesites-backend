A playbook holds four things: the steps of the call, the questions that decide whether a lead is worth your time, the objections you already know you will hear, and the line that says when to hand the call to a person.

One playbook, two readers

What a rep sees

While the call is live, the right-hand rail shows the current step and the next one. They type an objection into a box and the answer appears. The qualification questions sit there as a checklist, and ticking one files the answer against the lead.

What the AI is told

The same steps become the AI's instructions, the same questions become the things it has to find out before the call can end, and the same objection answers become what it says back. The transfer line becomes the rule for when it hands over.

Write it once and both sides stay in step. This matters later: when you change a line because it is not working on live calls, the AI changes with it the same day, instead of drifting a month behind your best rep.

Ask the cheapest disqualifying question first.

The best scripts find out in the first thirty seconds whether this call can possibly end in a sale. If you only sell to people who own the building, ask whether they own the building before you say anything about the product. A no at thirty seconds costs you a cent. A no at eight minutes costs you the next three calls you did not make.

How to write a playbook that survives a real call

The steps

Five or six, no more. Each one is a sentence a person could actually say out loud. A script nobody can read at speed gets abandoned on call four and then you have no data about whether it worked.

  1. Who you are and why you called. One sentence. Name, company, the reason.
  2. The disqualifying question. The cheapest one, first. See above.
  3. The problem. One question that gets them talking about the thing you fix.
  4. What you do about it. Two sentences, tied to what they said.
  5. The ask. A specific time, not “sometime next week”.

The questions

These are the facts you need before a lead is worth a meeting: budget, who decides, when they are buying, whatever matters in your trade. Keep it to three or four. Each answer is saved onto the lead, so nobody has to re-ask it later and your reports can count them.

The objections

Write down the five you hear every week and the answer that works. Not the clever answer — the one that works. This is the part reps use most and the part that improves fastest, because you can see in the transcripts which answers end the call.

Transfer criteria

One plain sentence describing who is worth interrupting a rep for. “They own the building, they have had a leak in the last year, and they want someone out this month.” The AI uses this literally, so vague wording produces vague transfers.

What never to do

There is a field for this. Put the promises you cannot keep in it: prices you will not quote on a first call, guarantees you do not offer, claims a regulator would object to. The AI is told not to say them under any circumstances, and the post-call scoring flags a rep who did.

Start from a real call

The fastest way to write the first one is to make ten calls yourself with nothing but a notepad, then write down what you actually said. A playbook invented at a desk reads like it was invented at a desk.
